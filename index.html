Keith Crabb

Ph.D. Student, Computer Science & Engineering Graduate Researcher, Texas A&M Cybersecurity Center

I work on the security of systems that were never designed to be attacked by modern adversaries — industrial control networks, satellite links, and the machine-learning pipelines increasingly placed in front of both. My research builds measurement infrastructure and detection methods for these settings: high-fidelity digital twins for reproducible ICS intrusion-detection research, learned detectors for advanced persistent threats in operational-technology traffic, and protocol design for environments where the usual assumptions about connectivity and computation do not hold.

I am advised by Dr. J. A. “Drew” Hamilton at the Texas A&M Cybersecurity Center.

Research

Three connected threads: making industrial-control security research reproducible, applying machine learning to operational-technology threat detection, and hardening communication protocols against future adversaries.

01

ICS & SCADA Security

Intrusion-detection research on live industrial control systems is largely impractical: the equipment is safety-critical and attack traffic cannot be injected into a running plant. I build digital-twin simulation software that reproduces SCADA process and network behavior faithfully enough to serve as a testbed for anomaly and intrusion detection, so detection methods can be evaluated against realistic attacks without touching production hardware.

[C1] [C2]

02

AI Security & Learned Detection

Operational-technology networks produce traffic with structure that general-purpose intrusion detection misses. I evaluate machine-learning detectors — gradient-boosted decision trees, fuzzy-hashing similarity measures — on advanced-persistent-threat identification in ICS networks, with attention to what these models actually learn and how they degrade when an adversary is aware of them.

[C1] [C3]

03

Quantum-Resilient Systems

Satellite and tactical communication links have long service lifetimes, constrained compute, and — in orbit — a radiation environment that corrupts the state cryptographic protocols depend on. I work on post-quantum protocol design for these constraints, where resistance to quantum adversaries and resilience to bit-level fault injection have to hold at the same time.
